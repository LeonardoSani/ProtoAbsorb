\begin{abstract}
Test-time adaptation (TTA) is typically evaluated by closed-set accuracy on shifted in-distribution (ID) data, but real deployments often process mixed streams that include out-of-distribution (OOD) inputs.
In such streams, entropy-minimizing TTA sharpens predictions on all samples, creating a direct tension with OOD detection, which relies on preserving low confidence on OOD inputs.
We introduce a \emph{paired contamination protocol} to quantify this tension under a \emph{frozen-detector evaluation contract}: we compare an \textbf{ID-only oracle} that adapts on ID samples only (diagnostic, not deployable) against \textbf{mixed-stream adaptation} on the same test streams, while evaluating a pre-deployed OOD detector not updated during adaptation.
The resulting \emph{paired gap} isolates the OOD-detection benefit lost to OOD contamination in the adaptation loss.
Across four OOD datasets, multiple ID fractions, and five detectors, mixed-batch entropy minimization frequently forfeits most of the OOD-detection gain available under gradient-clean adaptation, and in many tested settings leaves OOD detection worse than no adaptation.
A four-condition mechanistic control attributes the gap primarily to \textbf{OOD gradient contamination}, while exposing BatchNorm to OOD inputs \emph{without} including them in the loss is consistently beneficial---overturning the hypothesis that shared batch statistics are the culprit.
We replicate the effect at ImageNet scale and in sequential streams with non-stationary contamination.
A cross-method audit reveals that closed-set accuracy improvements can mis-rank methods for open-world OOD safety, motivating the paired gap as a required reporting metric alongside standard TTA benchmarks.
\end{abstract}
